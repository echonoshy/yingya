# Plan the visual story

Use for new videos or a change in narrative direction. Apply only the detail the
piece needs: a four-second loop can have one scene and one purposeful action.
Do not equate a short user prompt with a request for a low-effort result.

## Choose the treatment

Before listing scenes, resolve the creative target: what the viewer should
understand, feel, or want to do afterwards. Infer reversible choices from the
brief; disclose material assumptions. For example, inspiring a child to draw
calls for different content than teaching drawing techniques. Do not add lessons,
claims, a sales pitch, or a CTA that changes the user's purpose.

Choose a throughline that fits the material: a concrete example, a recurring
object, a question answered progressively, or a demonstrated change. Record a
short rationale in the existing plan. A narrative arc is useful for an explainer;
a logo sting or loop does not need an artificial problem/solution story.

## Design meaning before motion

For each substantial scene, describe the visible event that does the explanatory
or emotional work. Pick the mechanism from the content: a comparison, an object
changing state, a causal chain, progressive construction, a reveal, or a detail
within a retained whole. These are possibilities, not a mandatory variety quota.
Text-led pieces and restrained product demonstrations are valid when requested.

Define the most informative settled frame first. Then describe the path into it:
what is already present, what changes, what triggers that change, what the viewer
should notice, and what remains afterwards. Keep a dominant visual clear; do not
decorate a weak idea with unrelated ambient motion.

Store extra detail in existing scene records only when it makes execution more
specific. Useful optional fields:

- `viewerTakeaway`: the new understanding or feeling this scene contributes.
- `visualMechanism`: how the image expresses the idea beyond repeating the text.
- `settledFrame`: the most informative arrangement and intended focus.
- `beats`: ordered objects with `cue`, `action`, and `result`; optional estimated
  scene-local `startSeconds`/`durationSeconds` when timing can usefully be planned.
- `continuity`: what is retained from the prior scene and handed to the next.

Keep `motion` as a concise scene-specific summary for existing consumers. Do not
require new top-level manifests or replace existing IDs. Beat timestamps remain
estimates until synchronized to measured speech or authored silent timing.

For example, for an explanation of a queue: items accumulate at an entrance;
one item reaches the server; the waiting count falls as that item enters service;
the served item exits while the next advances. The same objects and count make
the process legible. “Icons appear with smooth transitions” leaves the main
design decision unresolved. Use the example's level of specificity, not its
content or choreography, for unrelated topics.

## Compose the whole video

Let scene boundaries follow changes in meaning or visual task, not each sentence
or a fixed number of seconds. A long scene may contain several meaningful beats.
Allow reading, anticipation, and resolution; continuous movement is not a goal.

Connect scenes through a retained object, geometry, question, or deliberate cut
when useful. Keep type, palette, and motion tone coherent while allowing forms
to change with their purpose. Repeated layouts and actions are appropriate for a
repeated task; otherwise reconsider a sequence of nearly identical scenes.

## Reconcile with assets and review

Before approval, identify required assets and feasible alternatives without
promising unseen detail. After approval and sourcing, inspect the actual assets
and refine framing, layout, and motion within the approved direction. Escalate
only a material change of scope or meaning, not routine implementation choices.

Before handing the plan to production, check for a concrete defect: an important
idea without a visible explanation, incompatible simultaneous events, an
unreadable information load, an unmotivated repeated treatment, or an ending
that does not fulfill the opening. Repair specific defects rather than expanding
the plan or adding effects to reach an arbitrary count.

During production, inspect a representative difficult scene before extending
the design. Compare composed settled frames across the video, then inspect
motion across meaningful state changes and transitions. Confirm the planned
visual events actually happen. A clean screenshot alone cannot prove a good
sequence. Keep this an internal check within the existing approval workflow.
